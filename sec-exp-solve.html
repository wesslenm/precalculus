    Section 2.4 Solving with Exponentials and Logarithms

    Objectives

    • Solve exponential equations using a variety of strategies.

      🔗
    • Solve logarithmic equations using a variety of strategies.

      🔗

    Where will this be used in calculus?

    Solving exponential and logarithmic equations can be useful when looking for some specific value, for example:
    🔗
    • Intersection points,
      🔗
      🔗
    • Maximum/minimum points,
      🔗
      🔗
    • Points of inflection.
      🔗
      🔗

    With our review of fundamental characteristics of exponential and logarithmic functions under our belt, we will now review how to use these characteristics to algebraically solve equations involving exponential and logarithmic expressions. To do this, we will make use of the fact that exponential and logarithmic functions are inverses of each other, and thus "undo" each other’s work, much like radical functions undo the work of power functions. By the end of this section, we should be comfortable with algebraically solving equations involving exponential and logarithmic functions, and understand what these solutions would represent graphically.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.1 Basic Exponential Equations

    We have already solved some equations in the previous sections of this chapter. Here we go back to the basics again, for review.
    🔗
    As we’ve seen earlier, logarithms and exponential functions can "cancel each other out" since they are inverses of each other. This is a similar relationship that a radical expression shares with a power function. Observe the familiar equation below:
    \begin{equation*} x^3=125 \end{equation*}
    Here, in order to solve for \(x\text{,}\) we would apply the inverse operation of a cube; the cube root:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[3]{x^3}=\sqrt[3]{125} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x=5 \end{equation*}
    A similar argument can be made to solve exponential and logarithmic functions. Consider the following exponential equation:
    \begin{equation*} e^x=100 \end{equation*}
    Just like with solving any other type of equation, here, we want to apply the inverse operation to solve for \(x\text{.}\) In the previous example, the inverse operation of a cube was the cube root. In this case, the inverse operation of the natural exponential function is the natural logarithm. Therefore, we will take \(\ln\) on both sides of the equation:

    Aside
    Study tip:
    🔗
    Remember that \(\ln\) is an operator, and has no actual value in and of itself. In this example, we would not say "multiply by \(\ln\) on both sides" as \(\ln\) has no numerical value on its own. The way to describe this step is to say that we "take" \(\ln\) on both sides. This is similar to how we would say "take the square root on both sides" rather than "multiply by square root on both sides.’’
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \ln(e^x)=\ln(100) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x=\ln(100)\approx4.605 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 2.34. Solve Exponential Equations.

    Solve each of the equations for \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. \(\displaystyle e^x=17\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle 2e^x=30\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\displaystyle 5e^{\frac{x}{4}-1}+7=10\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    1. \begin{equation*} \boxed{x=\ln(17)} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \begin{equation*} \boxed{x=\ln(15)} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \begin{equation*} \boxed{x=4\ln \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)+4} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. \(e^x=17\)
      🔗
      Here we can simply apply \(\ln\) to both sides:
      \begin{equation*} \boxed{x=\ln(17)} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(2e^x=30\)
      🔗
      Here we must be careful to not apply \(\ln\) too early. First move the coefficient 2, then apply \(\ln\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} 2e^x=30 \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} e^x=15 \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \boxed{x=\ln(15)} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(5e^{\frac{x}{4}-1}+7=10\)
      🔗
      Again, do not apply \(\ln\) too early. First remove all things on the `outside’, then take \(\ln\text{.}\) Then move the things that were `inside’ of the exponential.
      \begin{equation*} 5e^{\frac{x}{4}-1}+7=10 \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} 5e^{\frac{x}{4}-1}=3 \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} e^{\frac{x}{4}-1}=\frac{3}{5} \end{equation*}
      Now we are ready to `take \(\ln\)’ because there are no things outside of the exponential:
      \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{4}-1=\ln \left(\frac{3}{5}\right) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{4}=\ln \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)+1 \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} x=4\left(\ln \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)+1\right) \end{equation*}
      Be careful with the bracket in the above step. The \(4\) is multiplied by both terms on the right side:
      \begin{equation*} \boxed{x=4\ln \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)+4} \end{equation*}
      Note: \(x=4\ln \left(\frac{3}{5}\right)+1 \) would be incorrect, and may be the result of incorrect brackets.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.2 Logarithmic Equations

    Once again, let’s consider this familiar equation:
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt[3]{x}=2 \end{equation*}
    Here, in order to solve for \(x\text{,}\) we would apply the inverse operation of a cube root (i.e. cube both sides):
    \begin{equation*} \left(\sqrt[3]{x}\right)^3=2^3 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x=8 \end{equation*}
    Just like how a cube and cube root are both inverses of each other, the natural logarithm and natural exponential functions are also inverses of each other. In other words, we would use the natural logarithm to "cancel out" the natural exponential function, and likewise, we would use the natural exponential function to "cancel out" the natural logarithm. Consider the following logarithmic equation:
    \begin{equation*} \ln x=5 \end{equation*}
    To "cancel out" the natural logarithm, we must use the natural exponential function. We must be mindful of how we apply the natural exponential function, though. To correctly apply this step, we must raise both sides of the equation \textbf{as the power of \(e\)}:
    \begin{equation*} e^{\ln x}=e^5 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x=e^5 \approx 148.413 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Aside
    Study tip:
    🔗
    A common misconception to try to \textit{multiply} both sides of the equation by \(e\) in order to cancel out the logarithm. Again, this is not correct, since while \(e\) \textit{does} have numerical value, \(\ln\) does not, so \(e \cdot \ln\) is meaningless.
    🔗
